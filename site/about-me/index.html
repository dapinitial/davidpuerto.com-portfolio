To know where you're going; you have to know where you've been...

Since Flash, I've been fighting the good fight that designers should code. Not because engineers can't design, and not because designers need to ship production code alone, but because a decision made in the medium it ships in reveals more than a static Figma can.

Coding is not dead. Manually creating code might be. The judgment about what to build, for whom, and what must never regress is the part that got more valuable. That's the part I have spent two decades years learning to leave where the next person will find it.

How I work

Grow the next people who build the thing before it exists.

I have been the new person in six companies. The seventh is about the people behind me: giving them the route, the systems, and a standard that holds without me in the room.

Design is a decision about someone else's time, made visible early enough to be cheap to change.

Everything I have built is that sentence. The Preview Site made a PM's decision visible before engineering spent a sprint. The iViz wheel made a surgeon's one-handed grip the constraint before hardware was cut. The playbook's gate makes a design-system violation visible before a reviewer's afternoon is spent on it.

"This design process that designers have been taught, we sort of treat it as gospel. That's basically dead." Jenny Wen, Head of Design for Claude, Lenny's Podcast

The design process you were taught is dead.

Discovery, mock, iterate, handoff. I stopped working that way in 2020, when the prototype became the product's first draft. Build in the medium it will ship in, then decide.

Two kinds of design work now.

Supporting rapid execution. Pairing with engineers, real-time feedback, the last-mile polish. For me that was live-coding previews over Teams, engineers taking the TSX as the blueprint, and PRs against Azure source.

Short-horizon vision. A three-to-six-month prototype that points a team in a direction. For me that was Project Studio, and the fourth generation of the Preview Site.

Trust is built by shipping early and answering.

"What destroys trust is shipping something early and then nothing happening." My version: receipts, not promises. Twelve hours to twenty minutes. A gate that refuses below the bar. A forensic agent that emails the SQL.

Ambivalent about the surface.

I have helped teams build surfaces that are not screens: MCP servers over Druid and Postgres, skills that compose the newsletter or schedule the send, a companion that takes a sentence and refuses the wrong one, an agent that deploys from my phone. They are surpassing the interim UI patterns of the last decade.

The experience is moving out of the interface and into the definitions, and a designer's judgment has to move with it: what the agent may do, where a person must decide, and what must never regress.

Start with the people who have to decide.

On Personnel the deciders were a PM team choosing between dozens of workflow permutations. At Apple it was the senior leadership team reading charts every week in a medium that forgives nothing. I find out who decides, what they are deciding, and when it becomes expensive to change, before I open an editor. And I research the team the way I research the customer: their office hours, their channel, what they are measured on.

Ask the questions.

  1. What purpose does this serve?
  2. How is it better than what exists, and than the alternatives we looked at?
  3. What does it let people do that they could not do otherwise?
  4. How will we prove it worked?
  5. Who have we left out?
  6. What does it cost, in money and in time?
  7. Who will have to learn it, train on it, manage it and maintain it?
  8. Does it still hold up at scale?

Prototype in the medium it will ship in.

Not a picture of the product: a signed-in preview on real data shapes, with simulated events, so the PM can click through the workflow and the engineer can read the code. On Personnel the previews were TypeScript and React behind our own gateway, and engineers took the TSX and CSS as the blueprint.

Let the room argue with the thing.

I live-coded previews over Teams during the pandemic, and PMs changed decisions in the meeting instead of a sprint later. At Apple, bringing the existing charts in one to one is what made leaving Tableau feel safe. The argument is with the artifact, not with each other, and it is had once.

Write the judgment down.

A rule either has a check that measures it, or it is marked judgment. The playbook holds 118 rules with checks and 46 marked judgment. Its gate refused my own restyle four hundred times, because the mockup's 38px buttons would have lowered the kit's 44px touch floor. A skin may carry the language. It may not lower an accessibility rule. Four hundred refusals is a trade-off I chose: speed, for a floor that never moves.

Hand off a thing, not a recommendation.

Every state in the preview is a story in Azure DevOps, linked both ways: 85 stories from one data twin. Engineering receives states, rules and tokens instead of a link to a picture. A researcher with a brief gets a walkable, accessible preview in an afternoon, and the recommendation survives contact with the backlog.

Leave the route marked.

PeopleCard went into the shared core with its documentation. The Azure accessibility fixes went in with a wiki the next engineer could follow, and Robert Taft wrote to everyone involved. SuperComposer runs on forty-plus teams with owner and member roles, every team its own room and no mandate. The standard holds without me in the room.

I have been designing and building since Flash. Now I am a UX Engineer: prototyping in Preview Sites aiding in design decisions, research, and investigation, managing design systems, integrating, and now building factories that connect the cloud services, so that we have boilerplate in isolation passing component governance, which we can leverage to bootstrap new sites that adhere to the design language and the skills.
