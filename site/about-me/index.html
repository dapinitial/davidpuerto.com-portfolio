To know where you're going, you have to know where you've been.

Since Flash, I've been fighting the good fight that designers should code. Not because engineers can't design, and not because designers need to ship production code alone, but because a decision made in the medium it ships in reveals more than a static Figma can. Coding is not dead. Manually creating code might be.

The judgment about what to build, for whom, and what must never regress is the part that got more valuable. For two decades I have been learning to write that judgment down, in design systems, documentation, tests and now skills, so the next person, or the next agent, can pick it up without me in the room.

How I work

The design processes we were taught has evolved.

Discovery, mock, iterate, handoff. I stopped working that way in 2020, when the prototype became the product's first draft. In this landscape everything has to work and look perfect, and the decisions still have to be made quickly. So the job is to get the thing off the ground and into code while the decisions are still being made, and let the team decide against something real. Build in the medium it will ship in, then decide.

Two kinds of design work now.

Supporting rapid execution. Pairing with engineers, real-time feedback, the last-mile polish. For me that was live-coding previews over Teams, engineers taking the TSX as the blueprint, and PRs against Azure source.

Short-horizon vision. A three-to-six-month prototype that points a team in a direction. For me that was Project Studio, and Email Composer. Now I am automating redesigns and evolving design with the current iteration of Preview Sites. The next generation might be new surfaces entirely. Perhaps skills in the terminal people use to craft specialized software to solve specific tasks.

Trust is built by shipping early and answering.

"What destroys trust is shipping something early and then nothing happening." My version: receipts, not promises. Twelve hours to twenty minutes. A gate that refuses below the bar. A forensic agent that emails the SQL for people to investigate themselves when the data integrity comes into question.

Ask the questions.

  1. What purpose does this serve?
  2. How is it better than what exists, and than the alternatives we looked at?
  3. What does it let people do that they could not do otherwise?
  4. How will we prove it worked?
  5. Who have we left out?
  6. What does it cost, in money and in time?
  7. Who will have to learn it, train on it, manage it and maintain it?
  8. Does it still hold up at scale?
