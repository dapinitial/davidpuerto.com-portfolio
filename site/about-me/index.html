Since Flash

Since Flash, I have been fighting the good fight that designers should code. Not because engineers cannot design, and not because designers should ship production systems alone, but because a decision made in the medium it will ship in is the only one a room can argue with.

Coding is not dead. Manually creating code is. The judgment about what to build, for whom, and what must never regress is the part that got more valuable, and it is the part I have spent nineteen years learning to write down where the next person will find it.

The arc, in four moves

Microsoft, designer PM. Personnel for JEDI, then Cloud Screening and Gov Screening. Preview Sites for several JEDI teams: I was loaned across them, bootstrapping each PM team's ideals into one coherent design system.

Microsoft, the engineering seat. When JEDI was bound up in the courts and the re-orgs came, I was merged into a software engineering role on Sovereign Clouds. My mentor, Arash Motamedi, told me to take the growth mindset literally and learn Azure from the inside. I did.

Apple, Senior UI/UX Engineer in UX Engineering. I collapsed fourteen executive dashboards into one surface, rebuilt thousands of hand-tuned charts one to one from an ingestion pipeline, and turned the surface into a publishing system that forty-plus teams ran themselves, with Editor and Approver roles built in.

Since then, Unakin. Agentic systems built in the open: Panogram for spatial and 360° media, Spacelab Studio, and shipmate, an agent that deploys and changes DNS hands-free from my phone and refuses to execute unless a plan ran in the last ten minutes.

What design means to me

Design is a decision about someone else's time, made visible early enough to be cheap to change.

Everything I have built is that sentence. The Preview Site made a PM's decision visible before engineering spent a sprint. The iViz wheel made a surgeon's one-handed grip the constraint before hardware was cut. The playbook's gate makes a design-system violation visible before a reviewer's afternoon is spent on it.

How I have seen design teams work best

One shared artifact beats shared opinions. Judgment lives in the system, not in whoever is in the room. Clear roles let people run in parallel. Make being wrong cheap.

One shared artifact beats shared opinions. The 2013 Experience Team at SonoSite put industrial design, interaction design, human factors, mechanical engineering and one software engineer around one prototype. The arguments were with the thing.

Judgment lives in the system, not in whoever is in the room. The playbook's 118 rules and 473 tests. The test harness on the design system at Apple. Quality that survives a reorg.

Clear roles let people run in parallel. At Apple, editors drafted, approvers signed, schedulers delivered, in sandboxed collections so seven lines of business never crossed.

Make being wrong cheap. Plan before execute. Per-change undo. Drafts gated by row-level security. Teams take risks when rollback is one step.

Help a research-led studio ship what it learns. Research recommendations die between the readout and the roadmap, not because anyone disagrees with them, but because by the time there is capacity the recommendation is a slide and the context is gone. And the people delivering the experience get the tools too, not only the people receiving it.
