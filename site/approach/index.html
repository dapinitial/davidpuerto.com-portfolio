The guide

The route nobody else is on, and everyone can follow.

A bishop moves along the diagonal, the line the other pieces do not use. That is the guide's job: find the route the room cannot see yet, prove it is safe by walking it first in working code, and mark it so the next person can take it without me.

The guide

Climbers prepare for an ascent by setting objectives, strategizing with partners, mastering systems for mechanical advantage, staying physically ready, and watching the weather and the terrain change under them. I start every project the same way, and I expect to improvise on the way up.

My role as a UX engineer and product designer is nothing short of being a guide. The summit belongs to the people who have to decide. I set the route, carry the systems, read the conditions, and get everyone up and back, mountain after mountain after mountain.

The eight questions

Will our solution significantly improve the current experience? How can we empirically demonstrate it? What are the tangible and intangible costs? What is the learning curve, and how quickly can people adapt? How well does it scale? Who have we left out? What technical constraints must we overcome? Is this still the best solution?

These keep the work from being a technical exercise playing to my own design biases. The answer is an outcome with receipts, mindful of the jobs that must be done, the budget, the needs and the timeline.

Prototyping as the catalyst

It starts at a whiteboard with engineers and product teams, naming the jobs the people we are designing for need to carry out. We build off the most feasible wins, then hold scope with flows, scenarios, personas and wireframes before the browser takes over.

In the browser the prototype runs on real data shapes with simulated events, so the hypothesis is tested in the medium it will ship in. Prototypes are not disposable artifacts. Each iteration leaves reusable assets, and the design tools come after, turning what worked into editable deliverables, a language and a set of patterns.

Judgment in the system

This is where the approach has grown. The prototype used to be the hand-off. Now the judgment about it is written down too: a rule either has a check that measures it, or it is marked judgment, and there is no third option. The design language is data, worn by every product rather than copied, so the permutation that introduces the bug never exists.

That lets a design review happen in CI, a gate refuse a build that lowers an accessibility floor, and a second team adopt the standard without me in the room. The argument is had once and written where the next person will find it.

Design as empowerment

Bridging design and implementation is how people and organizations achieve more than they thought they could. My path as a designer and a software engineer taught me that the bridge is built from collaboration, relationships and trust, and that trust is earned by shipping something that runs.

Actions speak louder than words. We owe it to the people we design for that the work renders on any device, meets their basic needs, and can be relied on. That is the standard, and the craft I keep honing.
