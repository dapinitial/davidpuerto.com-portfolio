Start with the people who have to decide.

On Personnel the deciders were a PM team choosing between dozens of workflow permutations. At Apple it was the senior leadership team reading charts every week in a medium that forgives nothing. I find out who decides, what they are deciding, and when it becomes expensive to change, before I open an editor.

Ask the eight questions.

  1. Will it significantly improve the current experience?
  2. How can we empirically demonstrate it?
  3. What are the tangible and intangible costs?
  4. What is the learning curve, and how fast can people adapt?
  5. How well does it scale?
  6. Who have we left out?
  7. What technical constraints must we overcome?
  8. Is this still the best solution?

Prototype in the medium it will ship in.

Not a picture of the product: a signed-in preview on real data shapes, with simulated events, so the PM can click through the workflow and the engineer can read the code. On Personnel the previews were TypeScript and React behind our own gateway, and engineers took the TSX and CSS as the blueprint.

Let the room argue with the thing.

I live-coded previews over Teams during the pandemic, and PMs changed decisions in the meeting instead of a sprint later. At Apple, bringing the existing charts in one to one is what made leaving Tableau feel safe. The argument is with the artifact, not with each other, and it is had once.

Write the judgment down.

A rule either has a check that measures it, or it is marked judgment. The playbook holds 118 rules with checks and 46 marked judgment. Its gate refused my own restyle four hundred times, because the mockup's 38px buttons would have lowered the kit's 44px touch floor. A skin may carry the language. It may not lower an accessibility rule.

Hand off a thing, not a recommendation.

Every state in the preview is a story in Azure DevOps, linked both ways: 85 stories from one data twin. Engineering receives states, rules and tokens instead of a link to a picture. A researcher with a brief gets a walkable, accessible preview in an afternoon, and the recommendation survives contact with the backlog.

Leave the route marked.

PeopleCard went into the shared core with its documentation. The Azure accessibility fixes went in with a wiki the next engineer could follow, and Robert Taft wrote to everyone involved. Email Composer runs on forty-plus teams with Editor and Approver roles and no mandate. The standard holds without me in the room.
